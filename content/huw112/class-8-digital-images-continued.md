### Assignment 2: Mini-Comic or Photo Essay

Due Thursday, Oct 22

**Both**
- 3–8 frames (~5 is a good target)
- Same size for every frame: story 1080 × 1920 or carousel 1080 × 1350
- Beginning, middle, end
- Text required: captions or speech bubbles
- A frame can hold more than one image

**Comic**
- Characters + something happens
- Original imagery: drawn (Figma or any drawing app) or staged photos you take
- Existing characters OK, but draw something new in every frame
- Retelling an existing story is fine

**Photo essay**
- About something real
- Title frame + a caption on every frame
- You must credit any photos you did not take
- Anyone recognizable agreed to be photographed

### Demo: masks, speech bubbles, filters / blend modes

Follow along with any image:

- **Frame + export**
	- Make a frame: 1080 × 1350 or 1080 × 1920
	- Duplicate it so every frame is the same size
	- Select all frames → Export → JPG or PNG
- **Photo in a shape**
	- Draw a rectangle or rounded shape
	- Fill → Image → pick your photo
	- Use this for panels and multi-image frames
- **Speech bubble**
	- Draw an ellipse + a small triangle for the tail
	- Select both → **Union**
	- Type your text on top
- **One blend mode**
	- Draw a colored rectangle over the photo
	- Change the layer's blend mode to **Multiply** or **Overlay**
	- Tints the whole frame, so photos shot at different times look like the same world

Try it: one frame with a photo in a shape, a bubble, and a color overlay. 



### Homework

1. 3 draft frames by Oct 15
2. Read the chapter *In Plato's Cave* by [Susan Sontag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Sontag), an American writer and cultural critic: 
https://www.are.na/block/14080779

Think about the following questions, and answer in Brightspace: 

1. Digital tools make it easy to alter, filter, or entirely fabricate images, do you think photos provide "proof" of what's real? Give an example from your own experience where you couldn't tell or were fooled about what was "real".

2. Sontag says photography created a new "ethics of seeing", an unspoken set of rules about what is worth photographing and who has the right to do so. How have those rules changed, since the advent of smart phones, social media, and pervasive surveillance? 

3. Sontag argues that photographing something can become a substitute for actually experiencing it. Think about how people document and in some cases live stream every moment of their lives. Do you think constantly taking pictures gets in the way of genuine experience, or does it enhance it?

4. Sontag wrote at a time when a photograph meant a camera had pointed at something that genuinely existed. Now images can be heavily edited, filtered, or generated entirely from scratch on a computer from a text prompt. Does the origin of an image — whether it was "real" "generated by a computer" — matter to you?
